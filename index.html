BBD · Platform Engineer screening

Platform Fit Check

Answer plain-English questions about a CV and get a score out of 10, a verdict, how close they are to the client's "ready to contribute" bar, and the questions to ask next. Built from BBD's benchmark CVs, every recorded interview outcome, and senior platform guidance.

What changed in this version 23 Sep 2026
  • The client's actual stack is now in the model: Kubernetes, Crossplane (written in KCL) and Argo, on AWS, Azure and Databricks, with Go / TypeScript / Python code.
  • Crossplane counts as infrastructure-as-code and is the closest match to the day-to-day work. Terraform is still good, but no longer the only way to pass.
  • Azure is as good as AWS. Don't mark someone down for being Azure-only.
  • Certifications are a small supporting signal only. CKA, CKS and CNPE count; KCNA and vendor certs barely move the score.
  • New: multi-team / multi-cluster scale, AI platform work (serving other teams, not being the AI team), a "ready to contribute" checklist, the closest past profile.
Plain-English glossary tap to open
Kubernetes ("K8s")
Runs and looks after many small programs at once, restarting or scaling them automatically.
Cluster
A group of computers working together as one system, managed by Kubernetes.
Terraform / IaC
Writing code that builds cloud infrastructure automatically, instead of clicking around by hand.
Crossplane
Manages cloud infrastructure the same way Kubernetes manages software. The client platform is built on it.
KCL
The language the client uses to write Crossplane "compositions" — the recipes teams order infrastructure from.
XRD / Composition
Crossplane's way of defining a menu item (XRD) and how it gets built (Composition). Writing these is platform work.
AWS / Azure / GCP
The three biggest cloud providers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google. The client uses AWS and Azure.
Databricks
A data and AI service the platform sets up for its data teams.
GitOps / ArgoCD
The "master plan" lives in code; Argo applies it automatically to the clusters.
Helm
A packaging tool for installing software on Kubernetes.
Self-service platform
Lets engineers get what they need on their own, without a specialist.
CI/CD
Automated pipelines that test and release code. Everyone does this now — it isn't platform work on its own.
RBAC
The rules for who is allowed to do what inside a system.
Operator / controller
A small Go program that teaches Kubernetes to manage something new. Strong platform signal.
CKA / CKS / CNPE
Hands-on Kubernetes exams done on a live cluster. KCNA is the multiple-choice entry-level one.
DevOps vs Cloud vs Platform
DevOps supports teams' releases; Cloud builds the cloud itself; Platform builds a product other teams self-serve from.
Ops vs platform
Ops keeps existing systems running (tickets, monitoring, admin). Platform builds something other teams use on their own. Same tools, different career story.
Bootcamp transitioner
Moved into tech via a short course, not years of hands-on work.
"Buzzword" CV
Impressive-sounding tools and claims, with little real detail behind them.
Strict-tier account
Some clients want juniors already doing platform work, with Python and Go — not people transitioning in.

How to fill this in: start with the CV, then close gaps in a short call — ask them to walk you through one real project. If you still can't tell, pick "Not sure" rather than guessing; the tool lowers its confidence instead of guessing for you.

Step 1

The basics

Who they are and which role you're weighing them for.

Which account is this for?
Which role?
What's the difference?
Platform Engineer: builds a self-service product other engineering teams use. Cloud Engineer: builds and secures the cloud infrastructure itself. DevOps: supports teams' pipelines and releases — broader and more junior-friendly.
Does their rate seem high for their level?
Step 2

The deciding question

Past panels have declined strong CVs on this alone.

Do they build the platform, or deploy onto one?
How do I tell?
The panel's test: someone who only knows how to deploy and maintain workloads on a platform is a DevOps engineer, not a platform engineer. Platform engineers build the platform itself.

Builds it: "built a portal / module / template other teams use", "developers can now create X themselves", a named internal platform, golden paths, Backstage. Deploys onto it: pipelines, releases, monitoring, incidents, tickets, "maintained clusters", lots of CI/CD talk. Building Jenkins pipelines is not platform work any more.

Ask: "Tell me about something you built that other teams use on their own, without you being involved each time."
Built self-service tools for other engineers?
Doesn't need the words "self-service" — does anyone get a server, pipeline or environment from something they built, without asking them?
Built it for many teams at scale? NEW
What counts?
Many teams sharing one platform safely (multi-tenant), rolling changes out to many clusters, onboarding new teams without tickets. Look for numbers of teams, clusters or apps — e.g. "monitoring for hundreds of apps across dozens of teams". Real platform work usually happens in big organisations.
Step 3

Technical signals

Judge by named projects, not the skills list.

Kubernetes
How do I tell?
Runs it: cluster upgrades, permissions (RBAC), writing operators/CRDs, multi-tenant setup, on-call for the cluster. Uses it: "deployed our app to Kubernetes", Helm installs, kubectl. Ask: "Who sets up and upgrades your clusters — you, or a team you deploy to?"
Crossplane NEW
Why this matters now
The client platform is built on Crossplane, with compositions written in a language called KCL. Someone who has written Crossplane "compositions" or "XRDs" is doing the actual day job — the strongest single match. Having it installed or "used" counts for less.
Other infrastructure-as-code (Terraform, OpenTofu, CDK, Pulumi)
Ansible alone doesn't count.
Cloud depth
Basic vs deep
Deep: permissions and identity (IAM / Entra ID, workload identity), networking (VPC / VNet, private endpoints), multiple accounts or subscriptions, security boundaries. Basic: launches servers and storage buckets. AWS and Azure count equally.
GitOps (ArgoCD / Flux)
Languages they've actually written code in
Go matters most — the client's platform tools are written in it. Being good at Bash is not impressive.
Anything else from this list? NEW
What these are
Backstage / developer portal: a website teams use to create things themselves. Policy guardrails: Kyverno / OPA rules that stop unsafe setups. Observability as a platform feature: monitoring that teams get automatically (Prometheus, OpenTelemetry). AI platform product: an AI/LLM gateway, model serving or agent sandbox offered to other teams — not building AI models themselves.
Certifications NEW
A small supporting signal only. Certifications back up real experience; they never replace it.
Step 4

Mindset and CV read

Curiosity is the #1 thing BBD hires for — ahead of any tool.

Do they show real investigative curiosity?
What it looks like
BBD looks for someone who can't leave a puzzle unsolved. Look for home labs, reading docs or source, comparing options before choosing, researching tech unprompted. Ask: "Tell me about something that broke and you didn't know why — what did you actually do?" "I restarted it" or "I asked someone" = mechanical.
Started as a software developer?
Currently in a platform / cloud / DevOps / SRE job?
Recent bootcamp or fast-track into tech?
Buzzwords, round numbers, or AI-sounding CV?
e.g. "reduced costs by 99%", long tool lists with no named project.
Really an app, AI or data developer with "platform" in passing?

Only the name is needed. Every answer you add makes the score more reliable.
